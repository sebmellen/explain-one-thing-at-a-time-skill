# Definition and Purpose of Abstracts

An abstract is a short summary of your published or unpublished research paper, usually about one paragraph—approximately **6–7 sentences** or **150–250 words**. A well-written abstract serves multiple purposes:

* It lets readers quickly understand the gist or essence of your paper or article so they can decide whether to read the full paper.
* It prepares readers to follow the detailed information, analyses, and arguments in the full paper.
* It helps readers remember the key points from your paper later.

Search engines and bibliographic databases also use abstracts, along with titles, to identify key terms for indexing published papers. What you include in your abstract and title is therefore crucial for helping other researchers find your paper or article.

If you are writing an abstract for a course paper, your professor may provide specific guidelines about what to include and how to organize it. Academic journals also frequently have specific abstract requirements. In addition to following the advice on this page, be sure to review and follow any guidelines provided by the course or journal for which you are writing.

# The Contents of an Abstract

Abstracts contain most of the following kinds of information in brief form. The body of your paper will develop and explain these ideas much more fully.

As the samples below demonstrate, the proportion of the abstract devoted to each type of information—and the sequence in which that information appears—will vary depending on the nature and genre of the paper being summarized. In some cases, certain information may be implied rather than stated explicitly.

The *Publication Manual of the American Psychological Association*, which is widely used in the social sciences, provides specific guidelines for abstracts covering different kinds of papers, including:

* Empirical studies
* Literature reviews or meta-analyses
* Theoretical papers
* Methodological papers
* Case studies

## Information Typically Found in an Abstract

Most abstracts include the following:

* The context or background of the research
* The general topic under study
* The specific topic of the research
* The central questions or statement of the problem addressed
* What is already known about the question
* What previous research has done or shown
* The rationale, exigency, or goals of the research
* The research or analytical methods used
* The main findings, results, or arguments
* The significance or implications of those findings or arguments

The rationale for the research may address questions such as:

* Why is it important to address these questions?
* Are you examining a new topic?
* Why is that topic worth examining?
* Are you filling a gap in previous research?
* Are you applying new methods to take a fresh look at existing ideas or data?
* Are you attempting to resolve a dispute within the literature in your field?

## Additional Guidelines

Your abstract should be intelligible on its own, without requiring the reader to read the entire paper.

You generally should not cite references in an abstract. Most of the abstract should describe:

* What you studied
* What you found
* What you argue in the paper

The body of the paper is where you should cite the specific literature that informs your research.
